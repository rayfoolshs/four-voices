# The Four Voices

Four poets, four ways of making a line. The app uses each as a **lens**: a set of
rhythmic habits and a root-language weather that you can borrow for a draft and
then put down.

The “pastiche” under each voice is **original writing in the manner of that
poet** — not a quotation. It exists so the scansion engine has something to
demonstrate on.

---

## At a glance

| Voice | Dates | Rhythm | Root stock | Comic / tonal note |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Seamus Heaney | 1939–2013 | loose iambic, 4–5 stresses, caesura | Old English, Old Norse, Irish | grave, tactile, unsentimental |

| Bob Dylan | b. 1941 | fourteener, rhymed couplets, internal rhyme | Hebrew scripture, Old English ballad | wry, biblical, restless |
| Brian Bilston | contemporary | strict form, plain diction, deadpan | Old English, Norman French, Latin for jokes | comic, domestic, exact |

## Seamus Heaney

**1939–2013 · Bellaghy, County Derry**
*The spade, the soil, the word.*

### Rhythm signature

> Heaney's line is a tool with mud on it. He counts beats more than feet, packs
> the line with short physical words, and lets a caesura fall where the spade
> hits. Alliteration is not decoration — it is the sound of two hard things
> touching.

- **Beat over foot.** Four or five stresses a line, with unstressed syllables
  crowded into the gaps.
- **The caesura as a tool-mark.** A comma, a dash, a full stop mid-line: the
  place where the work pauses.
- **Monosyllables carry the weight.** *clay*, *cold*, *sod*, *hand* — plain
  words struck together.
- **Enjambment with resistance.** The sentence pulls against the break like roots
  against a blade.
- **Hard consonants in company.** d, g, b, k — digging, cutting, lifting.

### Root languages

> Heaney's diction is a core sample. The bedrock is Old English — short,
> physical, monosyllabic. Old Norse supplies the northern weather. Irish dialect
> words are left deliberately unsmoothed, so the poem sounds like the place it
> comes from.

| Word | Root | Note |
|---|---|---|
| `loam` | OE *lām* | clay, mud; the ground itself |
| `clabber` | Irish *clábar* | mud, puddle; dialect kept, not translated |
| `scraw` | Irish *scráth* | a sod or strip of turf |
| `brae` | ON *brá* | brow, hillside; a Norse slope |
| `ghost` | OE *gāst* | spirit, breath; older and stranger than “spirit” |
| `word` | OE *word* | speech, command; the poem's own material |

**Takeaway:** when a line goes abstract, reach for a tool, a body part, or the
name of the ground.

### Original pastiche — “The Spade”

```text
The spade goes down through a seam of loam
and turns a cold tongue in the earth's mouth.
My father's hands are in the handle's grain —
a smell of clay, a shiver of small stones,
the whole field breathing under the blade.
```

Five stresses, a mid-line pause, and the hard d/g/b consonants doing the digging.

### Practice

1. **Dig one line.** Choose an object a parent or grandparent used. Describe it in
   four monosyllables and one long word. Put a caesura where the tool strikes.
2. **Sound the consonants.** Write a line about work where every stressed word
   begins with d, g, b or k. Then remove one so the line can breathe.
3. **Root it.** Take three abstract words and replace each with an Old English
   physical equivalent — or with an object that does the same job.
4. **Place-name poem.** Six lines made only of place-names and what happened
   there. No adjectives. Let the names carry the history.

## Amanda Gorman

**b. 1998 · Los Angeles, California**
*The breath, the room, the we.*

### Rhythm signature

> Gorman writes for the breath and the room. Her line is long, deliberate, built
> to be spoken while a crowd breathes with it. Anaphora is the engine; the line
> break is the moment the argument turns, not the moment the sentence ends.

- **Anaphora as scaffolding.** *We have not…*, *We will…* — repetition is
  structure, not filler.
- **Long lines, three stresses deep.** Twelve to twenty syllables, often three
  heavy beats, delivered in one breath.
- **Tricolon.** Things arrive in threes; the third one lands.
- **Abstraction colliding with image.** *justice* next to *a bridge*, *grief*
  next to *a door*.
- **The public “we”.** The poem is a speech the reader is already standing
  inside.

### Root languages

> Her grandeur is Latinate: justice, liberty, republic, dignity. Her sense of
> form is Greek: rhythm, lyric, metaphor. And underneath the public address runs
> a scriptural cadence — the prophetic *and the people shall*.

| Word | Root | Note |
|---|---|---|
| `justice` | Latin *iustitia* | the root of the civic register |
| `liberty` | Latin *libertas* | freedom as a public condition |
| `dignity` | Latin *dignitas* | worth, rank, the right to be seen |
| `democracy` | Greek *dēmokratia* | rule of the people |
| `rhythm` | Greek *rhythmos* | measured flow; the body's argument |
| `jubilee` | Hebrew *yovel* | the year of release; a prophetic word for repair |

**Takeaway:** Latinate nouns give the poem its public weight; plain images keep it
from floating away.

### Original pastiche — “The Sentence”

```text
We have not come this far across the dark to fold the map.
We have not carried every name this far to lose the door.
We are the sentence that the silence kept against its teeth —
a country learning how to say its name out loud.
And if the name is broken, we will break the silence
into syllables of light and hand them back.
```

Anaphora, long breath-lines, a tricolon arriving late, abstraction held by an
image.

### Practice

1. **The litany.** Five sentences beginning *We have not…*. Cut the weakest.
   Break the rest where the argument turns, not where the sentence ends.
2. **Tricolon drill.** Take one plain statement and give it two siblings — one
   smaller, one larger. Let the third carry the emotion.
3. **Civic image.** Put one abstraction and one ordinary object in the same line.
   Do not explain the join. *Justice* and *a bus stop*.
4. **Stand and breathe.** Read it standing. Mark every breath. Cut one word per
   breath without losing the sense.

## Bob Dylan

**b. 1941 · Duluth, Minnesota**
*The train, the hymn, the refusal.*

### Rhythm signature

> Dylan's engine is the old ballad line: fourteen syllables, seven beats, rhymed
> couplets, and a voice that arrives a half-beat late. He can make a sentence
> walk like a freight train and stop it on a dime.

- **Fourteener.** Seven stresses a line, often split 4 + 3 — the meter of
  ballads, hymns and nursery rhymes.
- **Rhyme as propulsion.** Couplets and abcb; the rhyme is the next station, not
  the destination.
- **Internal rhyme and catalogue.** The line rhymes inside itself and keeps
  moving.
- **Biblical cadence.** *And it shall come to pass* — King James rhythm under the
  blues.
- **The refusal.** One line that breaks the pattern to say what the pattern
  cannot.

### Root languages

> The King James Bible is the ground bass: jubilee, prophet, sabbath, amen. Old
> English ballad words keep the line walking. American vernacular keeps it
> honest. The legal-religious Latin of the pulpit surfaces whenever the song
> wants authority.

| Word | Root | Note |
|---|---|---|
| `jubilee` | Hebrew *yovel* | release, the trumpet year |
| `prophet` | Hebrew *navi* | the one who speaks for another |
| `amen` | Hebrew *amen* | so be it; the end-stop of the hymn |
| `sabbath` | Hebrew *shabbat* | rest; the seventh day as structure |
| `highway` | OE *hēah + weg* | the old road word, still walking |
| `rain` | OE *regn* | weather as refrain |

**Takeaway:** the rhyme is the engine, the scripture is the chassis, and one
plainspoken line keeps it from becoming a sermon.

### Original pastiche — “Midnight Freight”

```text
The whistle blew at midnight and the highway hummed with rain,
I stood beneath the station light and counted out the train.
The days come up like freight trains, the nights go down like stone,
and every road I travelled on keeps calling me back home.
```

Fourteener couplets, a biblical-prophetic sweep, and a plain last line that
refuses to be fancy. The `stone / home` pair is a near-rhyme — a useful teaching
example, not a mistake.

### Practice

1. **Headline to fourteener.** Take one news headline and make it a rhymed
   couplet of seven beats per line. Keep the grammar of the street.
2. **List song.** Ten lines of things seen from a bus, rhymed abcb, with one line
   that refuses to rhyme at all.
3. **Steal a cadence.** Take the rhythm of a hymn you know and put your own
   street, town and weather inside it.
4. **The refusal.** Write a strict rhymed stanza. Replace the last line with a flat
   sentence that tells the truth and does not rhyme.

## Brian Bilston

**Contemporary · England**
*Plain speech in strict clothes.*

Bilston is the pen name of a contemporary English poet often described as
“Twitter's unofficial poet laureate”. The app treats the voice, not the biography.

### Rhythm signature

> Bilston's trick is a collision: strict form, plain speech. A sonnet about a
> dishwasher. A villanelle about a group chat. The meter is immaculate, the
> diction is supermarket, and the joke is that the form takes the feeling
> seriously.

- **Form as straight man.** Sonnet, sestina, villanelle — the stricter the box,
  the funnier the thing inside.
- **Plain diction, exact rhyme.** No word you wouldn't text; no rhyme that isn't
  true.
- **Enjambment for the punchline.** The line break withholds the gag for half a
  second.
- **Deadpan tone.** The poem never winks; the reader does.
- **The title is part of the poem.** Often the setup, rarely the label.

### Root languages

> He keeps the plain Old English words and lets the Norman French and Latin in
> only when pomposity is the point. The comedy lives in the gap between the
> register of the form and the register of the sentence.

| Word | Root | Note |
|---|---|---|
| `kettle` | OE *cetel* | the domestic monosyllable |
| `agree` | French *agreer* | the language of terms and conditions |
| `perpetuity` | Latin *perpetuitas* | imported for comic pomposity |
| `soul` | OE *sāwol* | the plain word doing the real work |
| `wireless` | OE *wīr + lēas* | an old word for a new thing |
| `tea` | French *thé* | the borrowed comfort |

**Takeaway:** let the form be grand and the words be small. The friction between
them is the poem.

### Original pastiche — “Terms and Conditions”

```text
I read the terms and clicked agree,
a thing I did not read,
and now my soul belongs to them
in perpetuity.
I did not know I had a soul
to give away for free;
I only wanted fifteen minutes
of wireless, and tea.
```

Strict quatrains, exact-ish rhyme, deadpan tone, and a Latinate word used as a
punchline.

### Practice

1. **Appliance sonnet.** Fourteen lines on a household object. Strict iambic
   pentameter, real feeling, no puns in the rhyme. The form carries the joke.
2. **Villanelle of the group chat.** Two refrains, one of which is a message you
   would actually send. Keep the meter; let the banality do the work.
3. **The deadpan list.** A poem that is only a to-do list, with one line that is
   not. Do not flag the line.
4. **End plain.** Revise a draft so the last line is the plainest word in the
   poem. No summary, no flourish.

## How the profiles are used

The numbers above are **targets**, not definitions of the poets. For each draft
the engine extracts the same twelve features and computes a weighted, normalised
distance to each profile. The score is:

```text
distance = sqrt( Σ(weight_i · ((value_i − target_i) / scale_i)²) / Σ(weight_i) )
score    = round(100 · e^(−1.35 · distance))
```

The result is shown as “closest ear”, with the two features that came nearest.
It is a similarity reading, not a verdict. A poem can be 55% Heaney and 54%
Bilston — and still be entirely yours.

Full feature table and weights: [`ARCHITECTURE.md`](ARCHITECTURE.md#8-affinity).

## A note on imitation

Practising a poet's rhythm is old and honourable. Publishing a close imitation as
your own voice is neither. The pastiches here are deliberately *about* the
manner, not reproductions of any poem. Steal the cadence; keep your own life.
